Rotor angle

Why take the sensor off at all?

The encoder is an extra part bolted to the back of the shaft, and it is usually the first thing on a drive to fail. Removing it means:

  • one fewer part, one fewer cable, one fewer connector to shake loose
  • nothing to misalign when the motor is rebuilt
  • it survives heat, dirt and vibration that would kill an encoder
  • cheaper, and the motor can be shorter

The catch is the whole investigation. A back-EMF sensorless drive cannot infer angle at standstill, so this model uses an encoder-assisted startup before handing control to the estimator. The present sweep found sustained sensorless operation from 300 to 1500 rpm at 0.5 N·m; the 200 rpm case fell back to the encoder.

So the question is not "does sensorless work". It is where it works: at which speeds, and under how much load.

Where these numbers come from

Only the current at right angles to the magnets makes torque, so holding a given torque with an angle error ε needs current I/cos ε, and the copper loss 1.5·R·I² therefore rises as 1/cos² ε. Once that current reaches the 25 A limit the drive simply cannot hold the torque any more, and the shaft slows.

The back-EMF the estimator has to work from is ωe·λ, which falls in proportion to speed: about 11 V at 1500 rpm and 0.4 V at 50 rpm on these parameters. That is the physical reason a low-speed limit exists at all, and finding where it sits on this motor is the investigation's question.

What the sweep says

  • Electrical-angle RMS error rises from 0.345° at 300 rpm to 1.698° at 1500 rpm.
  • That trend corresponds to an approximately 63 µs fixed lag, an inference consistent with computational delay.
  • True shaft-speed RMS error stays around 0.0012–0.0015 rpm in the sustained cases.
  • Whole-run phase-current peaks sit near 7 A because the sensorless commissioning guard is active. The sensored side uses a 25 A ceiling, so current-limit comparisons are not yet fully like-for-like.

Still missing

The other load rows, finer points between 200 and 300 rpm, equalised current ceilings, hardware validation and a true standstill sensorless start. Until those exist, this is a one-load simulation result rather than the final operating map.

One BM1109 motor, one controller, one prescribed load torque. The chain below is the whole signal path, and every block in it exists in the Simulink model you can see on the rig tab.

Sensorless: where it stands

The sensorless model exists and works structurally. It aligns the rotor, accelerates it open loop, and hands over to its own angle estimate without the encoder ever entering the control path. What it cannot yet do is hold that angle accurately enough to be useful.

0 – 0.30 sfixed-field alignment, Id = 2 A, rotor pulled to a known angle
0.30 – 0.80 sopen-loop pull-in, Iq = 2.5 A, generated field ramps to 300 rpm
0.800 sobserver passes its validity gates; angle and current begin to blend
1.050 sblend complete, fully sensorless closed loop, no abort

That angle error is far too large for a usable drive. The handover sequence is sound; the estimator behind it is a simple voltage model and is the component that must be replaced before a sensorless speed–load envelope can be measured at all.

Does the instrumentation disturb the result?

Both models were run, then rebuilt with a shared measurement subsystem, then run again, and the two runs compared signal by signal. Across 16 logged signals in the sensored model and 34 in the sensorless model, the largest absolute difference was exactly zero, and all 64 summary metrics were unchanged. The measurement path is inert.

Words used on this page

Open the glossary
Field-oriented control (FOC)
Aiming the current in the windings at a particular angle relative to the rotor magnets, instead of just switching the windings on and off in turn.
Rotor angle
Where the magnets are pointing right now. Everything else depends on knowing it.
Electrical angle
The angle as the controller sees it. This motor has three pole pairs, so the electrical angle turns three times for every turn of the shaft.
d and q
Two directions, measured from the rotor rather than the frame of the motor. d points along the magnets, q at right angles to them. Current on q makes torque; current on d makes only heat.
Id and Iq
How much current is on each of those directions. A working drive holds Id at zero and uses Iq to set the torque.
Back-EMF
The voltage a motor generates by being turned. It grows with speed, and it is the only thing a sensorless drive has to work the angle out from — which is why slow is hard.
Observer
The piece of code that estimates the rotor angle from the voltages and currents. The quality of the observer is what decides whether sensorless control works.
Duty cycle
The fraction of the time an inverter switch is on, between 0 and 1. At 1 the supply is used up and the motor cannot go faster, which sets the top of the operating envelope.
Encoder
A sensor on the shaft that measures the rotor angle directly. The thing this investigation is trying to do without.

What this work does not show

  • No torque was measured. The rig has no torque transducer. Torque is inferred as T = Kt·Iq, and the drive's own torque estimate uses the same constant, so it is not an independent check.
  • Kt is provisional. It comes from a manufacturer test point (3745.6 rpm at 48.059 V), which is weaker evidence than a direct back-EMF test. Six different maximum-speed figures for this motor are in circulation, from 3100 to 4200 rpm.
  • The battery is idealised. 26.5 V nominal is confirmed; charged voltage, cut-off voltage and BMS ratings are not. The model uses a perfectly stiff bus that neither rises when charged nor sags under load.
  • The inverter is averaged. No switching, no dead time, no device losses. Current and torque variation seen here is not PWM ripple.
  • No iron loss, no temperature. Efficiency figures are model-based and optimistic.
  • This is not ODrive. The sensorless estimator here is a generic back-EMF observer written for this study. ODrive's own estimator is a different algorithm and nothing here predicts it.
  • The motor itself is uncertain. The exact BM1109 variant is unconfirmed and the datasheet never states the back-EMF waveform, so whether it is strictly a PMSM or a BLDC is unknown.

Why this is hard, and why it matters

You are doing by hand what field-oriented control does continuously: keeping the stator current at right angles to the rotor magnets. Only the part of the current at right angles makes torque, so being ε degrees out costs you cos ε of it, and the rest turns into heat.

The controller manages it because an encoder tells it exactly where the rotor is, every 50 microseconds. Take the encoder away and it has to work the angle out from the voltage the magnets generate — which is what this whole investigation is about.

Why this matters

Id is current pushed uselessly into the magnets: it makes heat, not torque, and the controller holds it at zero. Iq is the torque-producing part. Both are steady numbers in the rotor's frame, so a simple controller can hold them — which is the whole idea of field-oriented control.

Everything here depends on knowing where the rotor is. The encoder on this rig measures it directly. A sensorless drive infers it from the voltage the spinning magnets generate, and that voltage shrinks with speed: about 11 V at 1500 rpm, but only 0.4 V at 50 rpm. That is why the investigation expects sensorless control to fail at low speed, and why the comparison is measured as a speed and load envelope.
