Why take the sensor off at all?

The encoder is an extra part bolted to the back of the shaft, and it is usually the first thing on a drive to fail. Removing it means:

  • one fewer part, one fewer cable, one fewer connector to shake loose
  • nothing to misalign when the motor is rebuilt
  • it survives heat, dirt and vibration that would kill an encoder
  • cheaper, and the motor can be shorter

The catch is the whole investigation. A back-EMF sensorless drive cannot infer angle at standstill, so this model uses an encoder-assisted startup before handing control to the estimator. The present sweep found sustained sensorless operation from 300 to 1500 rpm at 0.5 N·m; the 200 rpm case fell back to the encoder.

So the question is not "does sensorless work". It is where it works: at which speeds, and under how much load.

Honest about the limits

There is no torque transducer on this rig. Torque is inferred from the measured current using the motor's torque constant, and that constant is itself provisional, so torque figures carry that uncertainty. The encoder type, the chain pitch and the battery's charge behaviour are all still to be confirmed.

Where these numbers come from

Only the current at right angles to the magnets makes torque, so holding a given torque with an angle error ε needs current I/cos ε, and the copper loss 1.5·R·I² therefore rises as 1/cos² ε. Once that current reaches the 25 A limit the drive simply cannot hold the torque any more, and the shaft slows.

The back-EMF the estimator has to work from is ωe·λ, which falls in proportion to speed: about 11 V at 1500 rpm and 0.4 V at 50 rpm on these parameters. That is the physical reason a low-speed limit exists at all, and finding where it sits on this motor is the investigation's question.

That gap was tested, not assumed. Raising the simulated mechanical roughness fivefold brings the sensored current variation into the right range but gets the ordering backwards — rougher sensored in the model, rougher sensorless on the rig. What these numbers are not

  • The rig angle error is relative within each short capture. It omits the absolute initial offset, so it cannot be compared with the simulation's absolute error.
  • The rig current-controls the TEST motor and regulates speed on the LOAD motor. The simulation speed-controls one motor against a passive equivalent load. The two are compared metric by metric, never signal by signal.
  • Simulated current belongs to the speed-regulating motor; the passive load has no electrical model at all.
  • One session per mode. Repeat sessions would be needed before quoting a tolerance.
  • Motor parameters remain provisional, so absolute torque figures carry that uncertainty.

Still open

Low speed on the rig: the simulated boundary sits near 225 rpm and the hardware sessions start at 800. Until the rig is run down there, the low-speed limit is a simulation result only. A true standstill sensorless start remains undemonstrated in both.

The two sources of evidence

Measured

The rig, 10 October 2026. Two BM1109 motors on a chain, one controller each, one 26.5 V battery. One session per feedback mode, 800–1200 rpm, 21 matched points. The TEST motor is current-controlled; the LOAD motor regulates speed and its shaft is the independent speed measurement.

There is no torque transducer. Torque is inferred from current.

Simulated

One motor, one controller, a prescribed load. Speed PI every 500 µs into a dq current loop every 50 µs, inverse Park and space-vector modulation, an averaged lossless inverter and a surface-mount PMSM. Fixed-step solver at 25 µs, two steps per current-loop update.

Not the two-motor rig: one motor against a passive equivalent load.

Where they agree, and where they do not

  • The direction agrees. Both say sensorless costs current quality rather than speed regulation, and both stayed stable across the band that was run.
  • The magnitudes do not. The model is about 15,800× too smooth on current variation and 204,000× on shaft speed. It is not validated, and nothing on this site is a prediction of the hardware.
  • The cause was tested, not guessed. Raising simulated mechanical roughness fivefold brings sensored current into range but reverses the ordering, so the missing physics is electrical and in the active load drive.

The five things this cannot tell you

  • Whether sensorless works below 800 rpm on hardware. The rig never went there; the 225 rpm boundary is a simulation result.
  • Whether it can start from standstill. Startup is encoder-assisted in both.
  • How ODrive's own estimator behaves. The observer here is a generic voltage-model and PLL, written for this study.
  • Anything about torque ripple or efficiency in absolute terms, with no transducer and a provisional torque constant.
  • A tolerance on the measured figures. One session per mode is not a repeat.

You are the controller

Field-oriented control does one thing continuously: it holds the stator current at right angles to the rotor magnets. Only the part at right angles makes torque, so being ε degrees out costs you cos ε of it and the rest becomes heat. The game hands that job to you, at human speed, so you can feel why it is hard.

What it is really showing you

A real controller manages this because an encoder tells it exactly where the rotor is, every 50 microseconds. Take the encoder away and it has to work the angle out from the voltage the magnets generate — which is the whole of this investigation.

The game simplifies the motor on purpose: torque follows the sine of the angle between the field and the magnets, with inertia and friction, and nothing else. It is not a model of a PMSM, and it is not the simulation the results on this site come from.

The motor, seen from outside

The rotor's magnets spin. The current in the three windings must always push at right angles to them — that is what makes torque. The controller has to know where the rotor is to do this, and an encoder tells it.

Why this matters

Id is current pushed uselessly into the magnets: it makes heat, not torque, and the controller holds it at zero. Iq is the torque-producing part. Both are steady numbers in the rotor's frame, so a simple controller can hold them — which is the whole idea of field-oriented control.

Everything here depends on knowing where the rotor is. The encoder on this rig measures it directly. A sensorless drive infers it from the voltage the spinning magnets generate, and that voltage shrinks with speed: about 11 V at 1500 rpm, but only 0.4 V at 50 rpm. That is why the investigation expects sensorless control to fail at low speed, and why the comparison is measured as a speed and load envelope.
