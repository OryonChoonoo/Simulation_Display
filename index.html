Rotor angle

The question this rig answers

Field-oriented control needs to know where the rotor is. The encoder measures it directly. A sensorless drive estimates it instead, from the voltage the spinning magnets generate. This rig measures at which speeds and loads that estimate is good enough to replace the encoder.

Honest about the limits

There is no torque transducer on this rig. Torque is inferred from the measured current using the motor's torque constant, and that constant is itself provisional, so torque figures carry that uncertainty. The encoder type, the chain pitch and the battery's charge behaviour are all still to be confirmed.

The motor, seen from outside

The rotor's magnets spin. The current in the three windings must always push at right angles to them — that is what makes torque. The controller has to know where the rotor is to do this, and an encoder tells it.

Why this matters

Id is current pushed uselessly into the magnets: it makes heat, not torque, and the controller holds it at zero. Iq is the torque-producing part. Both are steady numbers in the rotor's frame, so a simple controller can hold them — which is the whole idea of field-oriented control.

Everything here depends on knowing where the rotor is. The encoder on this rig measures it directly. A sensorless drive infers it from the voltage the spinning magnets generate, and that voltage shrinks with speed: about 11 V at 1500 rpm, but only 0.4 V at 50 rpm. That is why the investigation expects sensorless control to fail at low speed, and why the comparison is measured as a speed and load envelope.
